Q1. Who are your users, and what changes for them?
My users are postgraduate students at a specialised business school, 900-plus across the School of Business. They sit outside the organisation, but the data is the school's own. Course coordinators and the programme office publish deadlines and weightage through the intranet and inside outline PDFs.
Right now a student assembles their academic picture from four places. Deadlines come from eLearn announcements. Weightage sits inside a course outline PDF. Group coordination happens in the cohort WhatsApp group. Then it gets re-keyed into whatever personal system they keep, which for me is Notion and a calendar. Nobody owns the ranking step, so all of us redo it separately, and weightage, the field the whole exercise turns on, is buried furthest from view.
TICK's first screen takes that step away. It puts weightage next to deadline and estimated effort. It does not tell anyone their deadlines; we know our deadlines. What it removes is re-opening a PDF to discover that the assignment I had filed mentally as small is worth 25%.

Q2. Augmented capacity and constrained capacity
Augmented. I had never read React. In about two hours I had a deployed app with screen switching and an animated completion state. Almost none of those two hours went into code. It was spent into working out the main screen, refining the second one with the next action, a three-step checklist and an estimated time, and then into wording and layout. Without the pairing there'd have been no app at all.
Constrained. Everything I caught, I caught by looking or clicking. Duplicated course-code and location boxes. A palette I never chose. An abbreviation, "PG Queue", that no outsider even myself would understand. Text clipped in mobile portrait. Tick Complete firing with nothing ticked.
What I missed had no appearance. TICK picks the most urgent task with one line in App.tsx: incompleteTasks[0]. There is no sort. Not by deadline, not by weightage, not by effort, which are the three fields the product exists to surface. It takes whichever row happens to sit first in tasks.ts, and it only looks correct because the model put the "Today, 23:59" task at the top. Reorder that file and the app shows the wrong task with no symptom. I did open the generated files, out of curiosity rather than instruction, and I could follow the surface of them: which file held what, and that the student name and number were hardcoded. What I could not follow was the logic. That is the real constraint. Reading the code gave me orientation, not judgment, and no amount of time in the preview panel would have closed that gap, because the preview renders the output of a wrong rule exactly as convincingly as a right one.

Q3. In the loop, on the loop, out of the loop
Two moments where my judgment changed the outcome. Prompt 7: "all the checklist box have to be ticked/mark to be able to click on the task complete button. Earlier i was able to complete the task without checking off any." The checklist it shipped was decorative, and only using the app revealed that. Prompt 8: "What does PG Queue means or represents?" where a first-time user would stall on it.
Where I was nominally in the loop and added nothing: the ordering logic. I read those files, was in the loop by any procedural definition, and still could not evaluate what I read. Being shown a diff is not reviewing it.
Forward, the line is who owns the information. Anything from the school's administration: weightage, deadlines, classroom and next-class details, keeps a human in the loop, however expensive. The app's credibility rests on it, and a misread percentage is invisible, the screen shows a confident number and the error surfaces at the grade.
Ticking a task complete belongs out of the loop: instantly reversible, visible at once, many times a week, and only the student who tapped it bears the error. Before signing that off I would want it measured that no completion is silently lost and that nothing downstream changes on a tap. Ordering can run on the loop with an occasional glance, plus a trigger: the same task or field flagged three times in a term stops being noise.

Q4. What did it build that you never sketched?
Exactly what the brief warned about. My guardrails said no Gemini, no outside service, no database. What shipped includes express and @google/genai in package.json, a metadata.json declaring MAJOR_CAPABILITY_SERVER_SIDE_GEMINI_API, and a .env.example expecting a GEMINI_API_KEY. Nothing in my app calls any of it. The kitchen was provisioned anyway.
I saw part of it during the build, the Gemini line in .env.example which I went looking for an actual .env, found none, and moved on. The capability declaration and the server dependency I only found while writing this.
It also decided things I did not know were decisions: row order, a footer, a toast, and what happens when the queue empties,  a completion screen with a reset button. There it was right where I was wrong, since I never specified an empty state.
To have caught it at the time I would have had to paste App.tsx back into the model and ask, in plain English, "what decides which task shows first?" I glanced at the files out of curiosity when I should have interrogated instead.

Q5. Learning pointers for the organisational context
	1	Before anything deploys, one person reads the dependency and capability files, not the code. My package.json gained express and @google/genai and my metadata.json declared a server-side Gemini capability, none of which I asked for and all of which is legible to a non-programmer in thirty seconds.
	2	Every ranking rule needs a named owner who can state it in one sentence. TICK's "most urgent task" was really "first row in the file", and nobody reviewing the preview would ever have seen it.
	3	Make every field on a screen name its source. TICK's weightages, deadlines and classroom numbers sit inside tasks.ts and display with total authority; in a real deployment each of those needs a system of record and someone accountable for it being right, or the screen manufactures authority it does not have.
